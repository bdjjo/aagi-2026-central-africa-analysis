# Full draft — Article option 1

## Central Africa's AI Governance Gap Is Now an Implementation Problem

*The 2026 Africa AI Governance Index shows six countries writing strategies
faster than they can build the institutions to deliver them — and why that
distinction should reshape the next round of policy.*

The first lesson from the 2026 Africa AI Governance Index (AAGI) reading of
Central Africa is not the one people expect. The story is not that the region
lacks AI policy. It is that policy is arriving faster than the machinery needed
to carry it out.

As the chapter lead who worked on this region, I want to be precise about what
the numbers do and do not say. The index assesses six countries — Cameroon,
Gabon, the Democratic Republic of Congo, the Republic of Congo, Chad, and the
Central African Republic — across eight governance pillars, each scored from 0
(no evidence) to 4 (advanced). The reported composite scores run from
approximately 0.2 for the Central African Republic to approximately 1.5 for
Gabon, the DRC, and the Republic of Congo. No country reaches the index's
*Established* threshold of 3.0 (Report p. 2).

![Figure 1 — composite ranking](../figures/png/01_composite_ranking.png)

That result is easy to misread as a league table of failure. It is not. The
index measures *publicly observable* governance capacity, and its own
methodology is careful about the point: a score of zero means the absence of
findable primary evidence, not proof that nothing exists. Read correctly, the
AAGI is a map of what can be seen and verified, not a census of everything
governments and institutions are doing.

### Decompose the composite, and the real finding appears

A single composite hides the most important pattern. When you break the score
into its eight pillars, the region's shape changes.

*Strategy and Vision* is the strongest regional pillar, with a six-country mean
of about 1.53. *Governance and Regulation* follows at about 1.48, and *Regional
Integration* at about 1.33. At the far end, *Implementation and Impact* averages
only about 0.57. Infrastructure, human capital, and innovation all sit below 1.0
on average (Report p. 2).

![Figure 3 — mean pillar scores](../figures/png/03_mean_pillars.png)

This is the finding that matters. It describes a familiar but consequential
sequence: a government can adopt a strategy, convene stakeholders, and sign a
regional commitment before it can show a funded institution, a working oversight
process, or a measured outcome. The data does not prove that strategies *cause*
weak implementation — I want to be clear that this is an association in a
cross-section of six countries, not a causal claim. But the distance between the
two dimensions is large and consistent, and it points to where the next round of
work has to go.

### Why the gap matters

AI governance becomes real at the point where an institution can act. A strategy
can name health, agriculture, education, or public administration as priorities.
Delivery is different: it needs a budget line, a responsible office, operational
guidance, a route for citizens to complain about an automated decision,
procurement rules that hold vendors accountable, and a habit of publishing
progress. Without those mechanisms, a strategy remains a genuine signal of
intent but a weak safeguard against harm.

The country tables make the gap concrete. Every one of the six countries scores
zero on several advanced indicators — AI safety evaluation capacity, bias-audit
requirements, AI-system registries, and health-AI device approvals among them
(Report p. 2). It is worth being fair here: the report itself notes that these
are globally nascent frameworks, not uniquely Central African failures. The
honest reading is that the region is missing formal operational safeguards that
much of the world is also still building — which is an argument for shared,
efficient capacity rather than for blame.

### Six countries, six pathways

The most important reason to distrust a single ranking is that similar composites
hide different capabilities.

![Figure 2 — pillar heatmap](../figures/png/02_pillar_heatmap.png)

Gabon, the DRC, and the Republic of Congo all sit at roughly 1.5, but for
different reasons. Gabon's strengths are in governance, ethics, and regional
integration; the DRC's are in strategy, infrastructure, and innovation; the
Republic of Congo leads the region in governance (2.0) and regional integration
(2.1) and hosts a notable regional research asset described in the report
(Report p. 2). Cameroon and the DRC share the highest strategy score in the
region at 2.1, yet their delivery scores remain low.

Chad is the clearest case for why profiles beat rankings. It records a
relatively strong *Governance and Regulation* score of 1.7 and a *Regional
Integration* score of 1.3, but only 0.4 on infrastructure and 0.1 on
implementation (Report p. 2). That is not simply a lower-ranked version of its
neighbours; it is a governance-capacity mismatch, where formal foundations sit
on top of very thin delivery capacity. The Central African Republic is different
again — near-zero across most pillars, in a context the report describes as
fragile and evidence-scarce. Placing Chad and the CAR on the same policy pathway
would be a mistake; Chad needs capacity acceleration, while the CAR needs
minimum viable institutions and basic evidence systems before advanced
governance is even measurable.

### The regional multiplier — and its limits

If national capacity is thin, regional cooperation is the obvious multiplier, and
the data supports treating it that way: *Regional Integration* is the region's
third-strongest pillar. The report also treats regional convention status as a
differentiator across these six countries (Report p. 2).

**[External context]** The wider policy architecture has moved quickly. The
African Union adopted a Continental Artificial Intelligence Strategy in 2024,
setting a continental frame for national action (African Union, 2024). And the
AU's Malabo Convention on Cyber Security and Personal Data Protection — the
continent's principal data-protection treaty — finally entered into force on 8
June 2023, once the fifteenth ratification was reached (African Union; CIPIT,
2023). These milestones give Central African states a shared legal and strategic
scaffold to build on. But a ratified treaty and a continental strategy are
themselves upstream signals; they do not, on their own, create the domestic
budgets, staffed authorities, and redress channels that the implementation
pillar measures. Regional action can reduce duplication and stretch scarce
expertise; it cannot substitute for a national institution that can act.

### The evidence problem behind the scores

There is a second lesson, and it is uncomfortable but important to state plainly.
The supplied data-collection workbook — the instrument designed to hold the 80
indicators behind the index — is only partially completed. It contains eight
populated indicator rows out of eighty: four in *Strategy and Vision* and four in
*Governance and Regulation*. The remaining six pillar sheets are blank, and the
supporting sheets for investment, public perception, and AI incidents hold no
substantive records (Workbook P1–P8; audit).

![Figure 7 — workbook completeness](../figures/png/07_workbook_completion.png)

This does not invalidate the report's six-country summary. But it does mean the
published results cannot currently be reproduced from the underlying workbook,
and I would rather say so than let the numbers carry more authority than their
evidence base supports. A transparent next release should include the completed
country workbooks or a normalised indicator-level file, the exact composite
formula, evidence dates, explicit missingness flags, and a clear separation
between "no evidence found" and "evidence of absence."

### What a zero really means

That last distinction is not a technical nicety; it changes the interpretation
of a low score. A zero can mean a law or institution is genuinely absent. It can
also mean a researcher could not find a reliable public record in a low-
transparency environment. Those two states call for different responses — the
first for policy action, the second for better disclosure and recordkeeping. An
index that blurs them risks misdirecting scarce attention. The AAGI's own
methodology draws the line correctly; the task now is to carry that discipline
into the data fields themselves.

### A practical agenda

The agenda that follows from all this is refreshingly concrete.

First, each country should translate its strategy into a published
implementation compact: named institutional owners, annual milestones, budget
commitments, priority sectors, procurement rules, an AI-system inventory
requirement, complaint and redress mechanisms, and monitoring indicators.

Second, organisations deploying AI — public and private — should keep an
inventory of their systems, purposes, data sources, risk owners, and escalation
routes. Governance that cannot see its own systems cannot oversee them.

Third, regulators should prioritise enforceable basics — data protection,
redress, and procurement controls — before advanced certification or registry
schemes. Foundations first.

Fourth, because every country scores zero on safety evaluation and audit
capacity, a shared regional facility for testing, risk assessment, and auditor
training would be more realistic than six separate national systems built from
scratch.

![Figure 8 — strategy versus implementation](../figures/png/08_strategy_vs_implementation.png)

The 2026 Central Africa report is valuable precisely because it makes the
implementation gap visible. Its next edition will be judged on movement in the
indicators that are weakest today: budgets disbursed, monitoring reports
published, safeguards operationalised, incidents documented, outcomes measured.

The question is no longer whether Central Africa has begun to take AI governance
seriously. It has. The more demanding question — the one this data puts on the
table — is whether those beginnings are becoming institutions that can act,
explain their decisions, answer for harm, and show results.
