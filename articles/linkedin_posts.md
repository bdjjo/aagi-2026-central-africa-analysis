## Post 1 — the headline finding

Central Africa's AI governance problem is not a shortage of strategy. It is the
distance between strategy and delivery.

Working on this region for the 2026 Africa AI Governance Index, the pattern that
stayed with me is simple: **Strategy and Vision is the strongest regional pillar
at about 1.53/4, while Implementation and Impact is the weakest at about
0.57/4.** Governments can adopt plans and sign regional commitments faster than
they can staff an oversight body, disburse a budget, or publish a monitoring
report.

That is not a failure of ambition. It is a signal about where the next round of
work belongs: institutions that can act, not more documents that describe
intent.

## Post 2 — read the index carefully

A low score in a governance index is a starting question, not a verdict.

**No country in the AAGI 2026 Central African sample reaches the index's
Established threshold of 3.0** — composites run from about 1.5 to 0.2. It would
be easy to read that as a scoreboard of failure. As someone who helped assemble
the evidence, I would caution against it.

The index measures what is publicly observable and verifiable. Its own
methodology states that a zero means "no findable primary evidence," not proof
that nothing exists. That distinction matters most in fragile and low-
transparency environments, where real activity often leaves no public record.

📊 Figure 1 — composite ranking against the 3.0 threshold.

When you use an index, do you check what its zeros actually mean? How should
indices communicate that to non-specialist readers?

## Post 3 — why profiles beat rankings

Three countries, the same score, three different problems.

In the AAGI 2026 data, **Gabon, the DRC, and the Republic of Congo all sit at a
composite of about 1.5** — but their strengths diverge completely. Gabon leads on
governance and regional integration; the DRC on strategy and innovation; the
Republic of Congo on governance and human capital.

This is the case I keep making to fellow practitioners: a ranking tells you where
a country sits, but a pillar profile tells you what to do next. Two countries can
share a number and still need opposite interventions.

## Post 4 — the case of Chad

Strong governance scores and near-zero delivery can sit in the same country.

Chad is the example I return to. **It scores 1.7 on Governance and Regulation but
only 0.1 on Implementation and Impact** — with infrastructure at 0.4. That is not
a smaller version of a higher-ranked country; it is a governance-capacity
mismatch, where formal foundations rest on very thin delivery capacity.

My takeaway from building this chapter: for a country like Chad, another high-
level strategy is not the priority. Infrastructure, skills, and a handful of
measurable public-sector pilots are. The prescription has to match the profile.

📊 Figure 2 — Chad's row on the pillar heatmap tells the story at a glance.

institutions meant to deliver it?

## Post 5 — transparency is part of governance

Reproducibility is a governance quality, not just an academic one.

Here is something I insisted on documenting honestly: **the supplied data-
collection workbook behind this analysis contains only 8 populated indicator rows
out of 80.** The architecture is right — eight pillars, ten indicators each,
evidence and source fields — but most cells are still empty.

That does not disprove the report's findings. It does mean the results cannot yet
be reproduced from source records. If we want indices that institutions and
funders can trust, the raw evidence, dates, and calculation formulas have to
travel with the headline scores.

## Post 6 — the regional opportunity

The region's next governance gains may come less from national budgets than from
shared capacity.

**Regional Integration is the third-strongest pillar in the AAGI 2026 data, at
about 1.33/4** — ahead of infrastructure, human capital, innovation, and
implementation. Every country, meanwhile, scores zero on AI safety evaluation and
audit capacity.

My argument as a Central African practitioner is that these two facts belong
together. Six countries building testing, evaluation, and auditor-training
capacity separately will move slowly. One shared facility — with common
inventories, incident taxonomies, and procurement standards — could move faster
and stretch scarce expertise across borders.
